Fitness & health basics

Six health numbers

You can track a hundred metrics. These six tell you most of what you need to know about where your health stands, and you can measure all of them at home this week.

01

Body mass index

BMI divides your weight in kilograms by your height in metres squared. It's quick and free, and it's a blunt tool. A muscular 90 kg lifter and a sedentary 90 kg office worker of the same height get the same score. Treat it as a first screen. Run your numbers through the BMI calculator, then check the next number to see what the score is hiding.

02

Waist-to-height ratio

Measure your waist at the navel after a normal breath out, then divide by your height in the same units. A 170 cm person wants a waist below 85 cm. This ratio tracks belly fat, the deep fat around your organs that drives heart disease and type 2 diabetes, and it predicts that risk better than BMI does. The guide to waist-to-height ratio shows what each band from 0.4 to 0.6 means.

03

Weekly exercise

The World Health Organization sets the floor at 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening work on two or more days. Moderate means you can talk but you can't sing. A brisk 30-minute walk five days a week covers the cardio. Here's a week that hits both targets:

04

Daily calories

Your total daily energy expenditure (TDEE) is the number of calories you burn in a day, counting your resting metabolism, your training, and everything you do on your feet. Eat at that number and your weight holds steady. Eat about 500 below it and most people lose roughly 0.5 kg a week. The TDEE calculator gives you a starting figure. Weigh yourself for two weeks and adjust from there, since every formula is an estimate.

05

Protein

The official minimum is 0.8 g per kg of body weight, which is enough to prevent deficiency in someone who sits most of the day. If you lift or you're eating in a deficit, aim for 1.2 to 1.6 g per kg to hold on to muscle. A 75 kg person training three times a week needs about 90 to 120 g, or roughly 30 g at each of three or four meals. Read how much protein you need per day for the numbers by goal and age.

06

Water and sleep

The US National Academies put total daily fluid at about 3.7 litres for men and 2.7 litres for women, and around a fifth of that comes from food. You need more on hot days and training days. Pale-yellow urine is a good sign you're drinking enough. The water intake calculator adjusts the target for your weight and activity.

Sleep is the cheapest recovery tool you have. Adults need 7 to 9 hours. Under 6 hours a night, appetite hormones shift and people eat more the next day, so poor sleep undoes good training and good eating faster than most people expect.

This page is general information for healthy adults. If you're pregnant, have a medical condition, or take medication, ask your doctor before changing how you eat or train.
